Halation, measured. What the glow does.

Abstract

Halation is light that passes through the film, reflects off the back of the base and exposes the film a second time from behind. We tested how Halo Pro 2.2 reproduces it with controlled light: test charts holding light from two stops under white to five stops over it, three rendered light scenes, and real photographs. In these tests the glow doubles with every stop above white, as on film, follows the colour of the light the way colour negative film does (red glows, blue does not), grows with the size of the light, starts in the light tones, stays off surfaces that are already bright, and scales with the film format. Around real lights it is a tight core with a soft tail; it does not yet draw the ring film makes around a pinpoint, and its default look was matched to a look made in an established film-emulation plug-in rather than derived from a base thickness. These and other limits are listed at the end. Every figure gives the settings it was made with.

1Halation on film

A colour negative is three light-sensitive layers on a clear base about a tenth of a millimetre thick. Blue is recorded on top and a yellow filter stops what is left of it; green and red go deeper. The emulsion absorbs most of the light on the way down, never all of it: a fixed share of every tone leaves it, scattered, into the base. At the back of the base, light that arrives more obliquely than the critical angle (about 42° for a refractive index of 1.5) cannot leave and is reflected back up; nearer straight down only about 4 % reflects, rising steeply toward the critical angle [5, 6]. It re-enters the emulsion from below and meets the red-sensitive layer first, which is why halation is red. Returning orange light exposes some green as well, and where the return is strong, red nears its limit while green keeps rising, so the strongest part of a halo reads orange [1].

Four things follow. Halation is proportional to the light: every tone makes some, but only lights many stops brighter than their surroundings make enough to see. Its colour follows the light: red reaches the base, blue does not. The distance it travels is set by the base: from a single point the light returns from about 0.23 mm out on 35 mm film, sharp-edged inside and fading outward, and around any real light wider than that the rings from all its points overlap into a bright core and a soft tail, so the gap only shows around pinpoints. And film stocks are made to prevent it, with an anti-halation layer or, on motion-picture film, a carbon-black backing called remjet; remove the backing, as CineStill does, and it comes back [3, 4]. The Light path section on the main page draws this step by step.

2Method

Test charts. Lights on black, drawn as 32-bit float TIFF files with sRGB primaries and tone curve and no embedded profile (so the app reads them as sRGB), able to hold light above white. The strip charts are 2400 × 600 px and the single light 1200 × 1200 px; at Format Super 8 a 600 px short side stands for the frame's 4.0 mm, about 7 µm per pixel. The charts hold: an exposure ladder from two stops under white to five stops over it, nine colours at the same peak (two stops over white in their strongest channel), seven sizes from 1 to 64 pixels in radius, a ramp of lights from 3.5 stops under white to white, the same light on black, mid grey and a bright surface, and a single small light for profiles.

Rendered scenes. A wet night street, a wall of neon rings in nine colours, and an interior with a window, candles and a lamp, built from simple shapes and rendered in Blender (Cycles) as linear light, with the brightest lights at roughly 40 to 120 times white. A camera or a scanner would clip most of that; a 32-bit file keeps it.

Processing. Every file was opened in Halo Pro 2.2 beta and exported with the app's own Export: halation only, Mist off. The settings are printed under each figure. The charts use Format Super 8, so the glow is wide enough to see at this page's size (at Super 8, Size 1 is as wide as Size 6 on 35 mm, where Smooth wide glow is partly active); the scenes use the 35 mm Default look and a stronger 16 mm look. Controls not printed are at their Default. Warmth on highlights, which warms bright surfaces in the Default look, is set to 0 for the charts so only the glow is measured.

Measurement. The plots compare each export with the chart that went in, in linear light: glow is what the app added. Each light's glow is summed in a ring around it, from 3 px outside its edge to about 100 px out; "share in green" is the green channel's part of that sum. The 16-bit exports clip at white within a few pixels of the brightest lights, so those plots slightly under-count the strongest glows. The brightness plot uses a 32-bit float export, which does not clip the glow at white; the others use 16-bit exports. The numbers behind each plot are in the Data table under it.

Where the Default comes from. The Default's width, strength, colour and start, and its Warmth on highlights, were matched to my own look in an established film-emulation plug-in, measured from its exported pictures, rather than derived from a base thickness. The controls follow the film; the starting point is a look I already used.

4Light scenes

The same behaviour in scenes. Each starts as a rendered image with its lights 40 to 120 times brighter than white, the way a scene is before a camera clips it. Switch between the light as it would be in a normal file and the result from Halo Pro.

5Photographs

Two digital photographs, exported from Halo Pro 2.2 at its Default look with Mist off. Both are ordinary 8-bit files, so their lights are clipped at white and the glow is set by Starts at, not by the true brightness of the lights. More photographs are being made for this note: neon, tail lights, sodium street lamps, a tungsten interior, a backlit portrait and sun through trees.

6Limits

  • No ring yet. The optics predict a ring at a fixed distance around a point of light (about 0.23 mm on 35 mm film), darker inside it. Around anything wider the rings fill in, which is what Halo Pro 2.2 draws (Fig. 5); it does not draw the ring around a pinpoint. A ring is on the roadmap.
  • A matched Default. The Default was matched to my own look in an established film-emulation plug-in, not derived from a base thickness (section 2).
  • Clipped files. Most photographs are 8- or 16-bit and clip their lights at white, so the glow cannot know how much brighter a lamp was than a white shirt (Fig. 1 shows the difference). Starts at stands in for the lost stops.
  • Colour spaces: fixed in 2.3. In 2.2 the glow used the file's own tone curve and its own red, green and blue, so the same scene in sRGB and ProPhoto glowed slightly differently. In 2.3 the glow and the mist look the same whether a photo is saved in sRGB, Display P3, Adobe RGB or ProPhoto RGB, and sRGB photos look exactly as before. Strong Effect, a 1.0 preset, still looks a little different on ProPhoto files: its Exposure, Contrast, Bloom and grain work on the file's own values.
  • Blocky edges on self-lit surfaces. Where a strong glow lands on a surface lit by the same coloured light, such as the wall inside a neon ring, 2.2 can leave blocky, speckled edges (faintly at the Default). Being fixed; Fig. 10 leaves its strong setting out until then.
  • Beta. Very fine diagonal stripes (blinds, grilles) can show faint bands in the glow, and very big glows (Size above about 5) can land unevenly on faces and trees.
  • Scenes are scenes. The rendered scenes are simple shapes with accurate light, not photographs; they show behaviour, not a finished look.
