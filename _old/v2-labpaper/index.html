01

What halation actually is —
and why it's hard to fake.

On film, bright light passes through the emulsion, bounces off the back of the base, and exposes the emulsion a second time from behind. The result is that warm bleed around highlights that everyone recognises and almost nobody can reproduce digitally.

The glow isn't painted on. It's a second, softer exposure from behind, and it happens only where the light was strong enough to get through.

So a real halation has four properties: a threshold (only bright enough sources bleed), a spread that behaves like light scattering in a base, a colour that starts red and drifts warmer in the brightest cores, and a roll-off so pure whites stay white. Halo Pro exposes each of those as a control instead of one "amount" slider.

Try it on the right: three lights of different brightness. Move the threshold and watch which ones earn a halo.

A toy version of what the app does per pixel — no blur passes, no edge detection, no colour mask. But the logic is the same: threshold picks the sources, spread is the base, dye is the emulsion, roll-off keeps the whites honest.

02

Why it has to be float.

A halo is a long, gentle gradient — from a bright core to nothing, over hundreds of pixels. In 8 bits that gradient has 256 steps to work with; after a few blur passes and a blend, the steps show. In 32-bit float there are no steps to show.

Honest note: your screen is 8-bit, so a true 8-bit-vs-float comparison would look identical here. The left image is quantised to far fewer levels to show the shape of the problem. In real files it appears as faint rings in dark gradients — and gets worse every time a plug-in rounds to 8 bits between steps. Float also means you can add halation anywhere in the workflow, not only as the last export step.

What float buys you, concretely.

  • 16-bit TIFF/PNG read at full depth, not truncated to 8In

04

Three honest ways
people use it.

Same engine, very different intentions. Being clear about which one you're after saves you an hour of slider-wandering.

the quiet one Analog

Match a stock.

You shoot digital but want the file to sit next to your film scans without looking like a stranger. Small spread, warm dye shift, roll-off on the whites. Nobody should notice the effect — only that the light feels right.

  • Start from a preset, then lower it
  • Halation only, mist off
  • Apply the LUT after halation
the physics one Realistic

Where light would bleed.

Backlit snow, headlights in rain, sun through trees, a window behind someone's head. Halation where the scene earns it, not everywhere. The threshold and edge tools exist for exactly this.

  • Raise the threshold until only true highlights glow
  • Use Sky Protection and the colour mask
  • Check the diagnostic views (keys 0–9)
the fun one Experimental

Break it on purpose.

Dye colours that don't exist, glow the size of the frame, lens smear that streaks in one direction, turbulent mist with a seed you can re-roll. The sliders stop where taste usually stops. You don't have to.

  • Turn on Advanced controls
  • Type values past the slider ends
  • Blend as screen, soft light or overlay
05

The sliders are a suggestion.
Type your own numbers.

Every value label in the app is clickable. Type a number, press Enter, and it's applied — even if it's far outside the slider's range. The slider just sits at its end and lets you carry on.

Simple by default · everything behind one switch

Out of the box you see Glow, Colour, Threshold and Highlight Roll-off for halation, and Density, Colour and Threshold for mist. Tick Advanced and the rest appears. Every group has a hover explanation in plain words; the physics lives in the tooltips.

Double-click any slider to reset it. Space toggles before/after. P renders an accurate full-resolution preview.

06

The processing line.

Like a roller-transport processor: the file goes in one end, passes through each bath in order, and comes out the other end with everything it arrived with.

In

16-bit stays 16-bit

TIFF, PNG and JPEG in. 16-bit files are read at full depth, converted to float32, ICC profile detected. ProPhoto, Adobe RGB and sRGB handled.

Lightroom

Edit In → Halo Pro

Right-click in Lightroom Classic, choose Halo Pro, work, press Save to Lightroom. The processed file lands next to the original in the catalog.

Batch

Same look, whole shoot

Open a folder, dial in one image, Apply to All, Export All. Up to 500 images per batch, exported on a background thread.

Out

Nothing lost

TIFF 8/16/32-bit float, PNG 8/16, JPEG. ICC embedded, EXIF, IPTC and XMP copied over with a bundled ExifTool. Keywords survive.
